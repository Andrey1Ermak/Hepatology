# aasld_2026_expo_biopsy — full-text note

- **Source:** Verna EC, Adler DG, Kappus MR, et al. AASLD Expert Practice Opinion (EXPO): Liver biopsy. Hepatology 2026, doi:10.1097/HEP.0000000000001828
- **Read:** 2026-11-21 from the full text (14 PDF pages; text pp. 1–12, references pp. 12–14). Pediatric section "Liver biopsy in children" and Table 4 on pp. 10–12.
- **Status of document:** AASLD **Expert Practice Opinion**, not a guideline: targeted literature review, **no systematic review or GRADE**, statements agreed unanimously by the writing group (p. 1–2). It states that it is an **update of the previous AASLD guidance "Liver Biopsy"** (ref. 5 = Rockey DC et al. Hepatology 2009;49:1017-44) (p. 2). → `supersedes: aasld_2009_biopsy` is justified; its evidence level is expert opinion.

## Answers to what was needed

1. **Pediatric section** (pp. 10–12, Table 4, statements 15–16):
   - Same indications, techniques and risks as adults, with infant-specific points. **Urgent assessment for biliary obstruction in cholestasis** is the most common indication for percutaneous biopsy in infants; electron microscopy matters more in neonatal cholestasis.
   - Genetic testing (panels/WES) has **largely, but not completely, replaced** wedge biopsy for suspected metabolic disease.
   - Infants: **transabdominal** (not transthoracic) approach; can be done at the bedside with minimal sedation (oral high-concentration sucrose + local analgesia). Toddlers, older children and most teenagers need sedation.
   - Percutaneous biopsy can be combined with a gallbladder cholangiogram in interventional radiology; most efficient when BA suspicion is low and the cholangiogram is normal.
   - **Automated needle throw length** by size: 1 cm for < 3 kg neonates, 2 cm for 3–20 kg, 3 cm for ≥ 2 years / 20 kg (text p. 11). Table 4 gives core length 1 cm if < 2.5 kg, 2 cm if 2.5–15 kg, 3 cm if > 15 kg; percutaneous needle 15–16 G.
   - **Transjugular** in infants/small children needs pediatric expertise; lower size limit **8–10 kg** (Table 4); useful with ascites, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia or when pressures are wanted. Small-gauge pediatric cores fragment and may lack enough portal tracts.
   - EUS-guided biopsy has been used safely in children (limited data).
   - Bleeding: in 275 biopsies in 190 children major bleeding was rare; **aspirin within 5 days did not raise bleeding risk**; **LMWH within 24 h before biopsy may raise risk**; needle size/number of passes not shown to raise risk, but limit passes in practice (p. 11).
   - **Statement 15:** consider biopsy in infants and children for unexplained abnormal liver tests, for fibrosis staging when noninvasive tests are uncertain/discordant, or when histology is needed for management.
   - **Statement 16:** choose the technique (wedge, percutaneous, transjugular, EUS) by tissue needed, age/size, sedation, anatomy, ascites/coagulopathy and local expertise.
2. **Minimum portal tracts:** retained. Current guidance: an acceptable specimen has **no fewer than 11 portal tracts**, and fewer than 11 complete portal tracts should be noted in the pathology report (p. 9). **Statement 12:** adequacy, including whether there are > 11 portal tracts, should be assessed and reported. **Statement 11:** percutaneous needle **≥ 16 G**; TJLB and EUS-LB minimum **19 G**; core length **> 1.5 cm** for all types. (AASLD 2009 asked for 2–3 cm; the new minimum length is 1.5 cm.)
3. **INR / platelet thresholds before biopsy:** **none given** (pp. 7–8). High-quality data are scarce; no clear difference in bleeding risk with vs without cirrhosis; **no specific PT/INR threshold defines high risk**; platelet cutoffs for transfusion are unclear (one study of 497 cirrhotic patients: prophylactic platelets did not change bleeding). The AASLD procedural-bleeding guidance classes percutaneous and transjugular biopsy as high-risk procedures but gives **no INR or platelet correction thresholds**. The authors **suggest**: with severe coagulation/platelet derangement, decide approach and prophylaxis case by case; with derangement, anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy, **transjugular is usually preferred**.
   - Drug holds for percutaneous biopsy (adult data): warfarin ~5 days to INR ≤ 1.5; DOACs 2–3 days (longer with renal impairment); clopidogrel 5–7 days; aspirin—no clear added risk in 3,636 patients. DDAVP only for uremia, weak evidence.
4. **Does it replace AASLD 2009?** Yes — explicitly an update of that guidance (p. 2).

## Other facts useful for cards

- ref_biopsy: image guidance (before or real time) for all percutaneous biopsies in adults (statement on imaging); ultrasound preferred.
- ref_biopsy: severe pain after biopsy is the strongest independent predictor of major complications (Italian study of 1,838 biopsies, HR 30.6) — watch for pain, hypotension, tachycardia, shoulder pain; most complications occur within the first hours (pp. 6, 9).
- ref_biopsy: a biopsy core is ~1/50,000 of liver volume → sampling error (p. 2).
- ref_biopsy (what to ask the pathologist): give the pathologist the clinical question in a standardized way; send for expert pathology review if ambiguous, discordant, rare, or if results drive transplant decisions (statements 13–14).
- ref_biopsy: sickle cell disease and amyloidosis carry higher bleeding risk with percutaneous biopsy (p. 6).
